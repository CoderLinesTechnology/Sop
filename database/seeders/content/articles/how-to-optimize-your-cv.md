Your CV is often the first document a reader looks at, and it sets the context for everything else in your application. Optimizing it does not mean inflating it. It means making true, relevant information easy to find, so a busy reader can see your strengths at a glance.

This guide focuses on CVs for university, scholarship and early-career applications, but the same principles apply to most job applications.

## CV or resume?

The terms are used differently around the world, so check what each application asks for.

| | Academic CV | Professional CV or resume |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | A full record of education, research and academic activity | A focused summary for a specific role |
| Typical length | Grows as your career develops | Often one to two pages early in your career |
| Common for | Graduate programmes, research roles, some scholarships | Jobs, internships and many programme applications |

In the UK and much of Europe, "CV" is the usual term for both. In the United States and Canada, a resume is typically a short document for jobs, while a CV is a longer academic record. Some European programmes and scholarships ask for a Europass CV, the EU's standard template; if yours does, use it.

## What readers look for

- **Relevance.** Information connected to the opportunity, placed near the top.
- **Evidence.** What you achieved, not just what you were responsible for.
- **Clarity.** Clear headings, consistent dates and a layout that is easy to scan.
- **Accuracy.** Details that match your transcripts, references and statement.

## A reliable structure

1. **Contact details.** Your name, email, phone number, and city or country. Add a professional profile or portfolio link if it is relevant and up to date. Conventions on photos, date of birth and other personal details vary by country, so include them only if the application or local practice calls for them.
2. **Profile (optional).** Two or three lines on who you are and what you are applying for. Make it specific or leave it out.
3. **Education.** In reverse chronological order: institution, qualification, dates, and your grade if it helps your case or is required. Add relevant modules, a thesis or a major project.
4. **Experience.** Work, internships, research and volunteering, most recent first, with two to five bullet points each.
5. **Skills.** Languages with your level (in Europe, CEFR levels such as B2 are widely understood), software, lab techniques or other tools you can genuinely use.
6. **Additional sections.** Awards, publications, presentations, certifications or activities, where relevant.

For academic applications, education usually comes first. For jobs where your experience is stronger than your studies, experience can lead.

## Write bullet points that show results

A strong bullet point starts with an action verb, says what you did, and ends with the result or purpose. Compare these illustrative examples.

**Before:**

- Responsible for social media.
- Helped with research in the lab.

**After:**

- Planned and wrote weekly posts for the student society's Instagram account, coordinating content with five committee members.
- Prepared and tested soil samples for a project on crop yields, and presented the findings at a departmental seminar.

The second set tells the reader what you did, at what scale and with what outcome. Use numbers only when you can stand behind them. "Coordinated a team of five" is fine if it is true; an impressive-sounding estimate you cannot support is not.

Useful verbs include organized, researched, designed, built, led, taught, wrote, presented, improved and negotiated. Choose the one that describes what you actually did.

## Tailor it for each application

You do not need a completely new CV for every application, but you should adjust it:

- Move the most relevant sections and bullet points higher.
- Cut or shorten items that do not support this application.
- Use the same terms as the job advert or programme description where they honestly describe your experience.
- Update your profile line so it reflects the opportunity.

Many employers use applicant tracking systems to sort applications. Standard headings, a simple layout without text inside images, and accurate keywords all help. Hidden keywords and copied job descriptions do not, and they can damage your credibility with the person who reads your CV next.

## What to leave out

Space on a CV is limited, so be selective:

- generic lists of qualities, such as "hard-working team player", without evidence
- school-level details once you have a degree, unless they are requested or still relevant
- references, unless the application asks for them; a line saying they are available on request is unnecessary
- personal details the application does not need
- anything you could not comfortably discuss in an interview

## Formatting checklist

- One clear, readable font in a sensible size, used consistently.
- Consistent date formats and alignment.
- Reverse chronological order within each section.
- Saved as a PDF unless the application asks for another format.
- A clear file name, such as "Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf".
- Links that work, and an email address you check regularly.
- No spelling errors: read it aloud, then ask someone else to read it.

## Keep it consistent with your other documents

Admissions and scholarship readers often see your CV alongside your statement, transcripts and references. Dates, job titles, grades and responsibilities should match across all of them.
