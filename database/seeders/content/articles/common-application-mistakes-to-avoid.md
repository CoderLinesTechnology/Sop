Many application mistakes have little to do with ability. They come from rushing, from writing for no one in particular, or from skipping the final checks, and they are usually easy to fix once you know what to look for.

Here are twelve common ones, grouped by where they happen, with a fix for each.

## Mistakes in your writing

### 1. Writing a generic statement

If your statement could be sent to any university with only the name changed, it is too generic. Readers want to know why this programme, and a generic statement cannot tell them.

**Fix:** refer to specific features of the programme that you have researched, and connect them to specific experiences of your own. Here is an illustrative example.

**Before:**

> I am applying to your prestigious university because it is one of the best in the world and I have always dreamed of studying there.

**After:**

> I am applying to this programme because its second-year placement would let me test what I have learned about supply chains inside a working organization, something my current degree does not offer.

The revised sentence gives a real reason and something for the reader to remember.

### 2. Not answering the question

Many prompts contain two or three questions in one. "Why have you chosen this programme, and how will it support your career goals?" needs two answers, and answering only the easier part leaves a gap the reader will notice.

**Fix:** list every part of the prompt, then check that your draft answers each one.

### 3. Listing achievements without reflection

A list of awards and activities tells the reader what you did, not what you learned or why it matters.

**Fix:** after each example, ask "so what?" and add a sentence that answers it.

### 4. Opening with a cliché

Famous quotations, dictionary definitions and lines like "ever since I was a child" are easy to write and easy to forget.

**Fix:** open with something specific to you: a question, a problem or the moment your interest became serious.

### 5. Exaggerating or inventing

It can be tempting to round up a role or add an experience that sounds impressive. Don't. Inaccurate claims can surface through references, transcripts or interviews, and they can cost you an offer or a scholarship.

**Fix:** describe your real experience accurately and let reflection do the persuading. A modest experience, described thoughtfully, is often more convincing than a grand one.

### 6. Copying from examples

Sample statements are useful for seeing structure, but borrowed sentences rarely sound like you, and some admissions services check submissions for similarity.

**Fix:** read examples for ideas about organization, then close them and write your own.

### 7. Getting the tone wrong

Too casual, and you can seem unprepared. Too grand, and you can seem insincere.

**Fix:** aim for the tone you would use with a respected teacher: clear, polite and direct.

## Mistakes in the details

### 8. Naming the wrong university or programme

When you adapt one document for several applications, it is easy to leave in the wrong name, or a module, city or professor that belongs to a different programme.

**Fix:** search each final document for every institution and programme name you have used, then read the final version once more.

### 9. Ignoring limits and instructions

Exceeding a word limit, missing a required section or uploading the wrong format suggests you did not read the instructions carefully.

**Fix:** check limits the way the application counts them, in words or characters, and follow every formatting instruction.

### 10. Letting your documents contradict each other

If your CV, statement and references give different dates, titles or responsibilities, it raises doubts about your accuracy.

**Fix:** read your documents side by side before submitting and make sure the details match. Our guide on [how to optimize your CV](/resources/how-to-optimize-your-cv) covers this in more detail.

## Mistakes in the process

### 11. Leaving it too late

Last-minute applications lead to rushed drafts, referees without enough time and no margin for technical problems.

**Fix:** set your own deadline a few days before the real one, and give referees several weeks' notice. If time is short, prioritize in this order: answer the prompt, get every fact right, then polish the language.

### 12. Skipping the final check

A tired final read misses things, and the wrong file is easy to upload.

**Fix:** proofread when you are fresh, ideally on paper or by reading aloud. Then open the exact file you are about to upload and check that it displays correctly, that formatting survived the conversion to PDF and that the file name is clear.

## A quick pre-submission check

- Every part of every prompt has been answered.
- Each document names the correct institution and programme.
- All limits and formatting instructions are met.
- Every claim is true and matches your other documents.
- Your referees have agreed and know the deadlines.
- You have saved a copy of everything you submitted.
