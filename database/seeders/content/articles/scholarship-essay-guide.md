A scholarship essay has a different job from an admissions essay. The committee is not only deciding whether you can succeed on a course; it is deciding where to invest limited funds. Your essay needs to show that you meet the scholarship's criteria and that the award would make a real difference to what you can achieve.

Most scholarships tell you what they are looking for. The skill lies in reading the call carefully and answering it with honest, specific evidence.

## Start with the scholarship call

Before you write anything, read every official document the funder publishes: the call for applications, eligibility rules, selection criteria, essay prompts, FAQs and any guidance notes. Write down:

- every question you must answer, word for word
- the word or character limit for each answer
- the selection criteria, in the funder's own words
- the funder's mission or purpose
- the required documents and the deadline, including the time zone

If something is unclear, check the official FAQ or contact the scholarship office. Second-hand summaries can be out of date, so do not rely on them for rules.

## What committees typically look for

Every scholarship is different, but committees commonly assess:

- **Alignment with the funder's purpose.** A scholarship created to develop future public-sector leaders wants something different from one supporting research in a particular field.
- **Achievement and potential.** Results matter, but so does evidence of what you could do next.
- **Initiative and leadership.** This does not require a formal title. Starting something, improving something or taking responsibility for others all count.
- **Impact.** What you have done for others so far, and what you plan to do.
- **A credible plan.** What you will study, why, and how the award makes it possible.
- **Financial need**, but only where the call lists it as a criterion.

## Map your evidence to the criteria

Before drafting, list each criterion and note your strongest evidence for it. The example below is illustrative; fill yours in from your own record.

| Criterion from the call | Your evidence | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership | Started a weekend reading club for local children | Initiative and follow-through |
| Academic potential | Final-year project on soil quality; strong results in core modules | Readiness for the programme |
| Community commitment | Two years volunteering with a youth sports charity | Sustained service, not a one-off |
| Plans after study | Return home to advise smallholder farmers on soil management | A specific, credible goal |

If a criterion has nothing next to it, think harder about your experience before deciding it does not apply. If it truly does not, consider whether this scholarship is the right fit.

## A structure for most prompts

1. **A specific opening.** Begin with a moment or problem that reveals what drives you, and keep it brief.
2. **Evidence.** Develop two or three examples from your map. For each, describe what you did, what changed as a result and what you learned.
3. **Your plan.** Explain what you will study, why this programme, and how the scholarship makes it possible.
4. **Longer-term impact.** Describe the contribution you hope to make. Ambition is welcome, but it should be believable.
5. **Close.** Connect your story back to the funder's purpose.

If the application has several shorter questions, answer each one directly and use different examples where you can, so the committee sees the full range of your experience.

### Illustrative before and after

**Before:**

> I am a natural leader who is passionate about making a difference in my community.

**After:**

> When our village library stopped opening on Saturdays, I started a reading club in the community hall. It began with six children. By the end of the year, two parents were running a second session, and I had learned that keeping a project going matters more than starting it.

The second version shows leadership instead of claiming it. It also shows reflection, which suggests you will learn from the opportunity as well.

## Writing about need or hardship

Some scholarships ask about your financial circumstances or obstacles you have faced. Share only what is relevant and what you are comfortable sharing. Be factual and brief, then focus on what you did in response and what the award would change. You do not need to dramatize your circumstances; a clear, honest account is enough.

## Practical tips

- **Answer the question asked.** A good essay on the wrong question will not score well.
- **Respect every limit.** Going over can count against you, and some portals will not accept the text at all.
- **Keep a master document.** Collect your strongest stories in one place, then adapt them for each scholarship instead of reusing whole essays.
- **Make every claim verifiable.** Committees may check references and transcripts, or invite you to interview. Everything in your essay must be true.
- **Get feedback early.** A teacher, a mentor or someone who has held a similar award can tell you whether your case is clear.
- **Submit before the final day.** Portals can be slow when many applicants submit at once.

## Final checklist

- Every prompt is answered fully.
- Each selection criterion is supported by at least one example.
- Your plan explains how the award will be used.
- All facts, dates and figures are accurate.
- The essay is within the limit and follows the required format.
- It has been proofread by you and by someone else.
