It is hard to write a strong application in a rush. Much of the difference between a calm, well-prepared application and a stressful one comes down to habits: knowing exactly what is required, starting early and checking the details. These ten tips apply to undergraduate, postgraduate and scholarship applications alike.

## 1. Build a requirements tracker

Before you write anything, make a simple spreadsheet with one row per application, and fill it in from the official pages rather than from memory or forums.

| Application | Deadline | Documents and limits | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| MSc Public Health, University A | 15 January, 17:00 UK time | SOP (1,000 words), CV, two references | Drafting SOP |
| Scholarship B | 1 December, 23:59 UTC | Essay (500 words), CV, transcript | Gathering evidence |

These entries are illustrative. A tracker like this turns a vague worry into a list of tasks, and shows at a glance what needs attention next.

## 2. Work backwards from your earliest deadline

Put every deadline in your calendar, then set earlier ones of your own: a date for a first draft, a date for feedback, and a date to submit a few days before the real deadline. Scholarship deadlines can fall before the deadline for the programme itself, so check both. Watch time zones too, since they can move a deadline by most of a day.

If your first deadline is about twelve weeks away, a workable plan might look like this:

1. **Weeks 1–2:** build your tracker, research each programme and contact your referees.
2. **Weeks 3–4:** gather your evidence and outline each document.
3. **Weeks 5–8:** write first drafts and revise them.
4. **Weeks 9–10:** collect feedback and edit.
5. **Weeks 11–12:** run your final checks, then submit early.

With less time, compress the plan, but keep the order.

## 3. Read every prompt like an examiner

Copy each question into your notes and underline every part of it. A prompt that asks why you chose the programme and how it fits your career plans has two parts, and both need an answer. When you finish a draft, go back and tick each part off.

## 4. Research each programme properly

Use the programme's official pages: the curriculum, teaching and assessment, entry requirements and application instructions. Look for specific features that connect to your interests. Forums and social media can suggest useful questions, but confirm the answers on official sources, because rules change.

## 5. Gather your evidence before you write

Make a master document of your experiences with dates: courses, grades, projects, jobs, volunteering, awards and responsibilities. Add a line on what you learned from each. Writing is much easier when you are choosing from a list rather than trying to remember everything at once.

## 6. Choose and brief your referees early

Ask referees who know your work well, and give them several weeks' notice if you can. Make their job easy: send your CV, a short summary of each programme, the deadlines and details of how the reference will be submitted. It is reasonable to remind them of specific work you did together, but the reference itself should be written by them.

## 7. Tailor every document, and keep the story consistent

Each statement should be written for the programme it is going to. At the same time, your CV, statement and references should agree with one another. If your CV says you led a project, your statement should not describe you as a team member on the same project. Dates, titles and grades should match everywhere.

## 8. Edit in stages and ask the right people

Leave a few days between drafting and editing so you can read with fresh eyes. Edit for structure first, then evidence, then language. Ask for feedback from someone who knows the subject and someone who does not, and give each of them a specific question, such as "Is it clear why I want this programme?"

## 9. Check the technical details

Small technical problems can cause delays or make a poor first impression. Before you upload anything, check:

- the required file format, often PDF unless stated otherwise
- page, word or character limits for each document
- clear file names, such as "Firstname-Lastname-SOP.pdf"
- that your name matches your passport or other official documents
- whether transcripts or certificates need certified translations

## 10. Submit early and keep records

Aim to submit a few days before the deadline. Portals can be slow near closing time, and an early submission leaves room to fix problems. Save a PDF of everything you submitted, keep confirmation emails and note your application numbers.

Submitting is not quite the end. Check the portal regularly for requests for further information, confirm that each referee has uploaded their reference, and re-read your statement before any interview. When decisions arrive, note the date by which you must respond to each offer.

## Bringing it together

None of these tips needs special talent. They need time and a plan. Start your tracker today, put the deadlines in your calendar, and the writing itself will feel far more manageable.
