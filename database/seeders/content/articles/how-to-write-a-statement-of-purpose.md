A statement of purpose (SOP) is a standard part of many graduate applications, especially in the United States, and programmes in other countries may ask for one too, sometimes under a different name. Its job is specific: to show an admissions committee what you want to study, why you are prepared for it, and why their programme is the right place to do it.

Where a personal statement often focuses on who you are, an SOP looks forward. It reads like a short academic and professional plan, supported by evidence from your record.

## SOP, personal statement or motivation letter?

Institutions use these names loosely, so always follow the prompt rather than the label. As a general guide:

| | Statement of purpose | Personal statement | Motivation letter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main question | What will you study, and are you ready? | Why this subject, and who are you? | Why this opportunity, and why you? |
| Often used for | Graduate and research programmes | Undergraduate and some postgraduate courses | Many European programmes, scholarships and exchanges |
| Emphasis | Preparation, interests, goals and fit | Motivation, experience and reflection | Motivation, fit and contribution |
| Format | Essay | Essay | Formal letter |

Some programmes ask for both an SOP and a separate personal statement. When that happens, they want different things from each, so keep the SOP focused on your academic and professional direction.

## What committees look for

Whoever reads your SOP has to judge whether you will succeed in the programme and make good use of it. They tend to look for:

- **A clear direction.** A specific area of interest, not a general wish to "learn more" about a broad field.
- **Preparation.** Relevant coursework, projects, research, methods and work experience, with evidence of what you achieved.
- **Fit.** Genuine links between your interests and what the programme offers: courses, research areas, resources and the interests of its academic staff.
- **Realistic goals.** A sense of what you will do with the degree, in the short and longer term.
- **Clear writing.** Your SOP is also a writing sample, so precision matters.

## Research the programme first

A strong SOP is built on research. Before drafting, spend time on the programme's official pages:

- the curriculum, including core and optional courses or modules
- research groups, centres or labs connected to the department
- profiles of academic staff whose work relates to your interests, and some of their recent publications
- the application instructions, including the prompt, length and any required sections

From this, choose two or three specific points of fit you can explain. If you mention a member of staff, make sure you have read some of their work, that it genuinely relates to yours, and that their profile is current. Never name people, labs or courses you have not verified.

## A structure that works

1. **Opening: your focus.** State the area you want to study and what drives your interest. A specific question or problem works better than a childhood memory.
2. **Academic preparation.** Describe the parts of your degree that matter most: key courses, a thesis or capstone project, methods you have learned. Say what you did and what you found.
3. **Research or professional experience.** Cover internships, research assistance, jobs or independent projects. Focus on your own contribution and what it taught you.
4. **Why this programme.** Connect your interests to the specific courses, research areas or resources you found, and explain what you would do with them.
5. **Goals.** Outline where you want the degree to take you. Be specific, but realistic.
6. **Close.** Briefly bring together your preparation, your direction and the programme.

If no length is given, write something a busy reader can finish in a few minutes; one to two pages is a common range.

### Illustrative example: showing fit

**Before:**

> Your university is world-renowned and has excellent faculty, which is why it is my first choice.

**After:**

> The optional course in spatial data analysis would let me build directly on my final-year project, in which I used open transport data to map gaps in bus coverage across my home city. I would like to extend that work to access to healthcare, a question that also runs through the department's current research on health inequalities.

The second version is specific, connects past work to future plans, and could only have been written by someone who had read the programme's pages. The details in your own SOP must come from your record and from information you have checked.

## Writing tips

- **Lead with substance.** Committees do not need your full life story. Start close to the work you want to do.
- **Be precise about methods.** Name the tools, techniques or approaches you used, and explain why they mattered.
- **Address gaps briefly.** If a career change, a weak semester or a gap year needs context, explain it in a sentence or two and move on. Some applications provide a separate section for this.
- **Stay measured.** Confidence comes from evidence, not adjectives, and flattering the institution adds nothing.
- **Tailor every version.** Changing the university's name is not tailoring. Each SOP should reflect what that programme actually offers.

## Checklist before you submit

- It answers the prompt and follows the stated length and format.
- Your research interests are specific and consistent throughout.
- Every course, research area or staff member you mention has been checked on the official site.
- Each claim about your experience is accurate and matches your CV.
- Your goals follow logically from your preparation.
- The programme and university names are correct in every version.
