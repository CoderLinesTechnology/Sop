A personal statement is your chance to show an admissions reader the person behind the grades: why you want to study this subject, what you have done to prepare, and what you would bring to the course. It is not a list of achievements or a life story. It is a short, focused argument, backed by evidence from your own experience.

This guide takes you from reading the brief to a final draft you can stand behind.

## What admissions readers are looking for

Priorities vary, but most readers are trying to answer a few simple questions:

- **Is the interest real and informed?** Readers look for curiosity that goes beyond the classroom, and an understanding of what the subject actually involves.
- **Are you prepared?** They want evidence: modules, projects, reading, work, volunteering or responsibilities that built relevant skills.
- **Can you reflect?** What you learned from an experience matters more than the experience itself.
- **Will you do well on the course?** Clear thinking, careful writing and realistic goals all suggest you will cope with its demands.

Notice that none of these questions is "Is this person impressive?" A modest experience described thoughtfully often says more than a long list of awards.

## Check the brief before you write

Requirements differ between application systems and institutions, so start with the official instructions. UCAS applications for UK undergraduate courses, for example, set a character limit rather than a word count and use their own structured questions, and the same statement goes to every course you apply for. US undergraduate applications usually ask for a personal essay in response to set prompts, while postgraduate programmes often set their own questions. Formats do change, so always check the current guidance.

Before drafting, note down:

- the exact prompt or questions, word for word
- the limit, and whether it counts words or characters
- whether the statement goes to one institution or several
- any formatting rules, such as plain text only

## Step 1: Gather your material

Before you write a single sentence, list anything that connects you to the subject:

- a moment, question or problem that first caught your interest
- books, articles, podcasts or lectures that changed how you think about it
- school projects, competitions, research or independent work
- jobs, volunteering, caring responsibilities or other commitments
- challenges you worked through, and what they taught you

For each item, ask: what happened, what did I do, and what did I learn? Your strongest material usually sits in the answer to the last question.

## Step 2: Choose a structure

This structure works for most courses:

1. **Opening.** Start with something specific: a question you want to answer, a problem you noticed, or the point at which your interest became serious. Keep it short.
2. **Academic preparation.** Show how your studies, reading and projects have prepared you. Be concrete about what you did and what you learned.
3. **Wider experience.** Add work, volunteering or activities that developed relevant skills, and explain the connection to the subject.
4. **Course fit and goals.** Explain what you want to explore on the course and where you hope it leads. If one statement goes to several institutions, write about the subject rather than any single university.
5. **Close.** End with a sentence or two that pulls your argument together. There is no need for a dramatic final line.

## Step 3: Write a fast first draft

Write the first draft without worrying about the limit. It is easier to cut a full draft than to expand a thin one. For each piece of evidence, use a simple pattern: say what you did, then say what it taught you and why it matters for the course.

### A short before-and-after

This example is illustrative. Yours should come from your own experience.

**Before:**

> I have always been passionate about engineering, and I am a hard-working and motivated student who loves solving problems.

**After:**

> When the rainwater tank at my school kept overflowing, I spent a term measuring the inflow with my physics teacher and redesigning the outlet. The fix turned out to be simple. Working out why the original design had failed was not, and that is the kind of question I want to study.

The second version never claims to be passionate or hard-working. It shows both, and it links the experience to the subject.

## Step 4: Edit in layers

Work through the draft several times, with one question in mind each time:

1. **Structure.** Does every paragraph earn its place? Could a reader summarize your argument in two sentences?
2. **Evidence.** Have you replaced vague adjectives with examples? Does each example come with reflection?
3. **Language.** Cut filler phrases, vary your sentence length and read the statement aloud to catch awkward rhythm.
4. **Limits and format.** Check the count the way the application counts it. Many portals accept plain text only, so bold, italics or bullet points may disappear.

Then ask one or two people for feedback, ideally a teacher who knows the subject and someone who does not. If both can explain why you want to study it, the statement is working.

## Pitfalls to avoid

- Opening with a famous quotation or a dictionary definition.
- Listing achievements without saying what they taught you.
- Claiming expertise you cannot yet have, such as a "deep understanding" of an entire field.
- Copying sentences from examples online. Some admissions services check statements for similarity with other submissions.
- Including anything that is not true, however small. You may be asked about it at interview.

For a longer list, see our guide to [common application mistakes](/resources/common-application-mistakes-to-avoid).

## Final checklist

- It answers every part of the prompt.
- It stays within the limit, counted the way the application counts it.
- Every experience mentioned is true and described accurately.
- Each example includes reflection, not just description.
- It focuses on the subject, and on one institution only if the statement goes to one place.
- It has been proofread on paper or read aloud at least once.
- You have read any declaration the application asks you to confirm.
