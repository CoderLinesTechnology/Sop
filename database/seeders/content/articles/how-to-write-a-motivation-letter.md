A motivation letter explains why you want a particular opportunity and why you are a good fit for it. Many universities in Europe ask for one as part of a programme application, and motivation letters are also common for scholarships, exchange programmes, internships and volunteering schemes.

The format is simple, but the letter has a lot to do in a small space, usually about a page. By the end, the reader should understand what you are applying for, why it matters to you, what you bring and what you plan to do next.

## What a motivation letter is, and what it is not

A motivation letter is a focused argument for your fit with one opportunity. It is not your CV rewritten in sentences, and it is not a personal essay about your childhood. Compared with a [statement of purpose](/resources/how-to-write-a-statement-of-purpose), it usually spends less time on research detail and more on motivation, fit and what you will contribute.

## What selection committees look for

- **Specific motivation.** Reasons that belong to you and to this opportunity, not general enthusiasm.
- **Understanding of the programme.** Evidence that you know what it involves and why it suits you.
- **Relevant preparation.** Studies, work or projects that show you are ready.
- **Contribution.** What you would bring to the group, the classroom or the organization.
- **Direction.** A credible sense of where this opportunity leads.
- **Care.** Correct names, a clear layout, the right length and no errors.

## Format basics

Unless the instructions say otherwise, keep the letter to about one page. If a word or character limit is given, it replaces any general advice, including this.

A conventional layout includes:

1. your name and contact details
2. the date
3. the recipient: a named person if the instructions give one, otherwise the admissions or selection committee
4. a subject line, such as "Application for the MSc in Environmental Management"
5. a salutation, such as "Dear Members of the Selection Committee,"
6. four or five body paragraphs
7. a closing, such as "Yours sincerely," followed by your full name

Letter conventions vary from country to country, and some portals ask you to paste text into a form rather than upload a file. Check the instructions, and when in doubt keep the layout simple, formal and consistent.

## A paragraph-by-paragraph structure

1. **Opening.** Say what you are applying for in the first sentence, then give your main reason in the next one or two. Make that reason specific.
2. **Your background.** Choose the two or three experiences most relevant to this opportunity. For each, say what you did and what it gave you.
3. **Why this programme.** Point to features you have researched: particular modules, a teaching approach, a placement, a research focus or a partnership. Explain why each one matters to you.
4. **Contribution and direction.** Show what you would add to the cohort, and how the opportunity fits your plans.
5. **Closing.** Summarize your case in a sentence, thank the reader, and say that you would welcome the chance to discuss your application.

### An illustrative opening

**Before:**

> I am very motivated and passionate about this programme, which I believe is the best in Europe.

**After:**

> I am applying for the Master's in Water Management because, after two years as a site engineer on drainage projects, I want to understand the planning decisions that shape a project long before construction begins.

In one sentence, the second opening tells the reader who you are, what you want and why. Use it as a model for structure only; your reasons must be your own.

## Tone and language

Write in the first person, in a tone that is formal but warm. Confidence should come from your examples rather than from adjectives about yourself. Avoid flattering the institution with words like "prestigious" or "world-class". The committee already knows its reputation, so this tells them nothing new about you.

If you are writing in a second language, choose clarity over complexity. Short, accurate sentences read better than long ones with errors. Ask a fluent reader to check grammar and tone, then make sure the final wording still sounds like you.

## Adapting the letter for scholarships and exchanges

For a scholarship, read the selection criteria and make sure each one is supported somewhere in your letter. Explain what the funding would make possible and how you plan to use what you learn. Our [scholarship essay guide](/resources/scholarship-essay-guide) covers this in more depth.

For an exchange, show what you hope to learn abroad, how it fits your current degree, and what you will bring back to your home institution.

## Checklist

- The letter names the correct programme, institution and intake.
- Your motivation is specific to this opportunity.
- Each paragraph has one clear purpose.
- Every experience you mention is true and matches your CV.
- It follows the stated length, format and file requirements.
- It ends with a polite closing and your full name.
- Someone else has read it for errors.
